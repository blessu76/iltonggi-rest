The first problem a map game has to solve is telling four kingdoms apart at a glance. If you can't tell who owns what, you can't read who has advanced where or which town is in danger, and a map you can't read offers neither thought nor fun. In Iltonggi, Goguryeo is red, Baekje blue, Silla yellow and Gaya green. The same colors run through flags, territories and borders. The first thing we did after choosing them wasn't to ask whether they looked nice, but to measure, with numbers, whether they could be told apart.

## Daytime is the easy part

Red, blue, yellow and green are four clearly different directions on the color wheel. On a bright daytime map, you can set them side by side and rarely mix them up. The trouble is that this gap shrinks when the lighting changes. Even the same colors grow alike as they darken, so knowing they were distinguishable by day wasn't enough to put us at ease.

There is a reason for measuring. What looks fine to the eye differs by person, by screen and by how you feel that day. Once you set a baseline, you can recheck against the same yardstick whenever you change a color, and even rank the riskiest pairs by number. It moves choosing colors from a matter of taste to a matter you can verify.

## Night is the hardest

Iltonggi's map shifts its mood to dawn, day, sunset, evening or night, following the current time. By day the colors are crisp, but at night a bluish glow covers the map and pulls the colors closer together. Under a blue veil, red and green both turn dark and murky and start to resemble each other.

When we measured the difference between two colors in a mockup, it was ample by day but only just above our baseline at night. The most precarious pair was red Goguryeo and green Gaya. A combination with no trouble at all by day was the first to blur once night fell. Barely clearing the line means it passed, but it also means that on a small phone screen with the brightness turned down, a slight change could make you miss it. So we didn't stop at "it passed."

## Not trusting color alone

So we decided not to trust the color of the land alone. Flags and borders are laid in their original colors over the darkness of night, and each kingdom's name is also shown on a hanja chip: 高, 百, 新, 加. Even when colors blur, letters and shapes should point the way.

Leaning on color alone could cause the same trouble in daytime too. Red and green are known to be an especially easy pair to confuse for people with color vision deficiency, and Goguryeo and Gaya happen to be exactly that pair. The idea of a map where not one color but color, letters and shapes speak together took hold here.

## How we draw soldiers

The battle screen draws a sky and soldiers. We chose to draw soldiers without faces or skin color, as ink-black silhouettes with only their kingdom's color laid on top. It's a choice meant to avoid, from the start, the distortions that can creep in when you simplify how people look. It means we thought about what kind of person even a tiny soldier sprite might seem to depict.

## What's still left

We haven't yet checked how well the map reads for people with color vision deficiency. We only suppose the hanja chips and shapes will help, and whether that's true remains to be confirmed. It stays on our homework list. Even as new maps or factions are added, the question we first asked will be the same: do these colors stay distinct at night, and on a small screen? What testers see when they open the map at night is the best check we could ask for.
