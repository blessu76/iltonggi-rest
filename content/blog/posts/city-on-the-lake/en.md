Act 8, "City on the Lake", is set in early sixteenth century Mesoamerica. It is the first act where we spent more time deciding what not to draw than what to draw. This post explains those choices. The act is still in development, and we are still reviewing it, so any of this may change.

## 1. A map built around water

The map has five nations and 27 regions. Its heart is a lake, drawn as a hole in the land, with island towns as small circles on it. The rivers to the west and south are there, and the volcanoes are shown as unnamed masses of peaks, because we did not want to put names on mountains that matter to living communities.

## 2. No feathers, no ritual objects

The soldiers wear plain quilted armor and a head cloth. We left out feathers, emblems, sacred objects and gods on purpose. We were worried that decorating a soldier with a pattern we do not fully understand would turn something meaningful into costume. The cost is that the soldiers look plainer than they might, and we know that being plain can also read as erasing, which is one of the questions we still want answered by people who know better.

## 3. No sacrifice, no conquest scenes

There are no scenes of sacrifice, conquest or disease anywhere in the writing or the pictures. We also did not use the modern country name, and we did not turn the great temple towns and sacred cities into regions. The sea beyond is just called the big sea, and the highland is just the highland.

## 4. A goal that is not a downfall

The goal is not to bring down the largest city. It is to hold a flag in Xochimilco, a flower-garden town on the south of the lake, for a round. We chose it so that the act would not be a story of one city's fall. Whether it is right to make a living place of cultural heritage the target of a game is a question we have written down for review.

## 5. People who were adapted

Several real people in this act died in the events of that era, and in our version they are alive for the length of the game. We tag these as adaptations in our notes, because making a tragedy lighter is a real cost. Only a few well-attested names appear, and the rest of the commanders are simply called by a title and a surname-style label, as general so-and-so.

## Finally

Names in languages that are still spoken today deserve to be written the way their speakers write them, and our spellings are not final. We would rather say that than pretend a transliteration is settled. If this act changes before you see it, the reason will most likely be one of the questions above.
